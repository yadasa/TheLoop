Clear enough to use.
Warm enough to welcome.

Write like a capable, considerate host: explain what people can do, respect their choices, and leave room for members to bring their own voices.

Scope: Edit system-owned navigation, buttons, onboarding, helper text, and loading, empty, and error states. Preserve member messages, posts, names, event titles, imported listings, and users’ grammar or dialect.

The writing rules

1. Name the thing

Use group chats, topics, direct messages, posts, replies, people, events, and photos consistently. Explain a branded name such as Squad once. Do not call both a post and a chat a “conversation” when the distinction matters.

2. Make actions predictable

Prefer Send, Create event, Join waitlist, Delete post. Keep the next step clear before someone taps. Remove decorative arrows from internal action names; reserve external-link cues for actual external destinations.

3. Keep warmth useful

A greeting or brief welcome can carry personality. Routine controls do not need a slogan. Repeated “a little,” “your people,” “thoughtful,” and “good plan” phrasing makes different screens sound alike and can conceal their purpose.

4. Describe the actual state

Distinguish loading, no results, missing access, and failure. Label bounded results as shown or loaded. Do not imply an exhaustive search, guaranteed identity, saved draft, successful delivery, or RSVP to an outside event unless the product confirms it.

5. Give errors a next step

Say what could not be completed and what someone can do next. Use calm language and a realistic recovery action. Do not invent a cause, blame the person, add a joke, or announce that a private item was deleted when its status is unknown.

6. Explain consequences

For deletion, cancellation, privacy, and invitations, keep the information needed to decide. “Cancel event” is clearer than “Yes.” Explain who can see a private group and which history a new member can access.

7. Respect a varied audience

Black audiences span ages, regions, industries, dialects, and reasons for joining. Use cultural context where it is relevant. Do not manufacture slang, intimacy, hustle language, or a single “Black professional” register.

8. Edit for the screen

Use familiar words, sentence case, natural contractions, short paragraphs, and descriptive accessibility labels. Preserve essential detail instead of enforcing arbitrary word limits. Read the string in its actual loading, error, and small-screen context.

What the research supports

These recommendations are editorial judgments informed by the sources below. Research on generated academic prose does not establish a universal “AI word list,” identify the author of an individual sentence, or validate every conclusion for app interfaces.

  • NN/g: ChatGPT and Tone · Taylor Dykes, March 8, 2024Experiments described in the article found exaggerated output when tone adjectives were used alone. Internal, approved examples, several alternatives, and human editing are more useful than asking for a vague persona. Do not ask the model to imitate a Blavity writer or celebrity.
  • NN/g: Product-Specific GenAI Needs to Write for the Web · Taylor Dykes, April 4, 2025The UX study supports concise, scan-friendly output with important information first and familiar language. For TheLoop, the control’s purpose should appear before any brand flourish.
  • NN/g: 7 Deadly AI Sins for UX Professionals · Tanner Kohler, September 26, 2025This practitioner article argues for human judgment rather than outsourcing taste. Treat generated copy as a draft to test against the task, not as a final brand decision.
  • Juzek and Ward: Why Does ChatGPT “Delve” So Much? · COLING, January 2025The study examines unusual word frequencies in scientific abstracts and possible reasons for them; its evidence about feedback training is qualified. FSU’s February 17 research report summarizes the work. It is not a detector for individual UI strings.
  • Terčon and Dobrovoljc: Linguistic Characteristics of AI-Generated Text · Research survey, October 2025The survey reports recurring linguistic tendencies, while noting concentration on English, GPT-family models, and insufficient attention to prompt wording. Avoid universal claims based on a single model, genre, or phrase.
  • Fleisig and colleagues: Linguistic Bias in ChatGPT · 2024 studyTests of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 found stereotyping and comprehension problems across dialects; imitation prompts were also problematic. This supports avoiding race-as-dialect prompts. It does not make members’ own language an editing problem.

Reading Blavity for context, not imitation

These articles show a range of reporting and personal perspectives. They are not representative audience research or proof that all Black readers prefer one tone. Borrow the attention to people, specific needs, and cultural context; do not copy sentences or perform an author’s voice.

  • First-time AFROTECH attendees describe their experiences · Aria Bell, November 21, 2024Attendees describe distinct reasons for attending, including recruiting, professional learning, and community. The reporting names people and concrete opportunities. TheLoop should likewise make specific activities easy to find without assuming every member has the same goal.
  • Is AfroTech Conference Worth It? · Lilian Ogbuefi, October 6, 2026The first-person account distinguishes hiring, learning, entrepreneurship, and social connection, including roles outside engineering. Our interpretation: professional usefulness and community enjoyment can coexist; neither should be treated as the only valid reason to join.
  • Issa Rae on support from organic relationships · Aria Bell, November 7, 2023The interview connects sustained relationships with practical mutual support. Our interpretation: invitations can be low-pressure and specific. The interface should not turn every hello into a pitch or presume closeness between strangers.
  • Am I doing this right? Racial politics at work · Ebony F., June 2016; updated March 2022This personal opinion piece addresses workplace pressure and code-switching. It demonstrates a different register from conference reporting. Our interpretation: respect boundaries, take safety language seriously, and avoid telling members how they must express their identities.

Product language to learn from

  • GroupMe: navigation and chat basicsIts help names concrete destinations and actions such as “Search chats” and “Start a new chat.” Use recognizable product nouns before inventing a metaphor for a destination.
  • Discord: Message RequestsThe documentation distinguishes “Accept” and “Ignore” and explains what content becomes accessible. The useful pattern is explicit consent and consequences, not copying Discord’s policies or implying TheLoop has the same features.
  • Slack: Create a channelThe mobile flow uses “Next” and “Create,” while explaining the public/private choice. Short controls work because the nearby context explains the decision.
  • Microsoft: Writing style for appsThe guidance supports familiar language, action-oriented controls, context-sensitive tone, and non-blaming errors with useful next steps. Warmth can be expressed through helpfulness rather than extra adjectives.

Review before release

  1. Can a new member predict what the button opens or changes?
  2. Does the same object have the same name across navigation, forms, confirmations, and accessibility labels?
  3. Does the string match the actual state, permission, result limit, and recovery action?
  4. Are destructive, privacy, invitation, and calendar consequences clear?
  5. Does the copy fit with large text enabled and make sense when read aloud?
  6. Were member content and imported source material preserved?
  7. Have members with different backgrounds and purposes tried the flow, rather than approving copy only in isolation?
